The big picture

What is this project?

Like you're fiveImagine a giant toy city. Kids in the city order pizza and ask for taxi rides all day long. Someone wrote down every single order in a giant notebook. This project reads the notebook and answers questions like "when is everybody hungry?" and "does rain make dinner late?"

Grown-up version: It is an end-to-end SQL analytics project modelled on Swiggy, Zomato, Uber and Ola across six metros: Bengaluru, Mumbai, Delhi NCR, Hyderabad, Chennai and Pune. It covers 90 days (Q1 2026). The data is synthetic, which means a computer program invented it with a fixed random seed. Busy lunch hours, rainy-day delays and surge pricing were built in on purpose, so the answers are interesting and the same every time. The numbers are not real company figures.

The point of it all

Why would anyone run it?

Like you're fiveIf you run an ice-cream truck, you want to know when the line is longest so you can bring more helpers. This project finds the "long line" times for pizza and taxis, and finds out what makes things slow, like rain.

1. Find the rush hours

Lunch and dinner carry almost half of all food orders. A company can put more delivery partners on the road at those times.

2. Find what slows things down

Rush hour adds about 6 minutes to a delivery. Rain adds 3 to 4 minutes. Bengaluru is the slowest city.

3. Test price rules

When taxi prices go up (surge), more riders get no ride. That helps decide if surge should be capped.

4. Show off SQL skills

Each query practises a technique: window functions, rankings, rolling averages, growth over time and anomaly detection. It works as a portfolio piece.

Tour of the house

What is in every folder, and what is it for?

Like you're fiveThink of the project as a house. One room makes the pretend orders, one room is the filing cabinet, one room holds the questions, and one room shows the answers as pictures.

The project root is Food Delivery Ride-Hailing Analytics\Food Delivery Ride-Hailing Analytics\. These are its contents.

README.mdThe instruction booklet

It is the page you read first. It says what the project is, how to start it, and what the answers were.

  • Run command, project layout, a table of all 14 queries, key findings, recommendations and limitations.
generate_data.pyThe toy-city factory

It invents the whole pretend city: every order, ride, restaurant and rainy day. It uses a "magic seed" number so it invents the exact same city every time.

  • Writes the seven CSV files into data/.
  • Builds in realistic patterns: lunch, dinner and commute peaks, weekend lift, rain effects, city traffic and partner productivity.
data/The raw notebooks (CSV files)

These are the long lists the factory writes down. Each file is one list, like a spreadsheet.

  • cities.csv – the 6 cities, with a traffic factor for each
  • restaurants.csv – 240 restaurants, their cuisine and how long they take to cook
  • delivery_partners.csv – 360 delivery riders
  • drivers.csv – 600 taxi, auto and bike drivers
  • food_orders.csv – about 80,000 food orders with times, fees, status and rating
  • rides.csv – about 49,000 ride requests with surge, wait time, fare and status
  • weather.csv – one row per city per day: rainy or not
sql/The rulebook for the filing cabinet

This room holds the plan for the cabinet and the questions you can ask it.

  • schema.sql – builds 7 tables with rules (for example a ride must be "completed" or one of three cancel types) and 7 indexes that make searches fast
  • queries/ – 14 files, q01 to q14. One question each, written in SQL
food_ride.dbThe filing cabinet (SQLite database)

All the notebooks, neatly put into one cabinet so you can ask questions fast. The pipeline throws it away and rebuilds it fresh on each run.

  • One single file. You can open it with DB Browser for SQLite or the VS Code SQLite extension.
run_pipeline.pyThe boss button

You press this one button and it does everything: makes the data, files it, asks all 14 questions and draws the pictures.

  • Uses pandas for tables, sqlite3 for the database and matplotlib for charts.
outputs/The answer sheets

Each question gets its own answer sheet here. The sheets are named after the question.

  • q01_kpi_summary.csv through q14_rain_impact_food.csv, 14 files in total
outputs/charts/The picture wall

Some answers are easier to see than read, so seven are drawn as pictures.

  • 01_hourly_food_peak.png – orders by hour of day
  • 02_daily_rolling_trend.png – daily orders with a smooth 7-day line
  • 03_city_delivery_time.png – median, mean and p90 delivery time per city
  • 04_weekday_hour_heatmap.png – hot and cold map of week and hour
  • 05_hourly_rides_surge.png – ride demand and surge by hour
  • 06_surge_vs_completion.png – does higher price mean fewer finished rides
  • 07_daypart_city.png – lunch, dinner and other times by city
__pycache__/Python's scratch paper

Python leaves little notes here so it starts faster next time. You can ignore it and even delete it.

Do it yourself

How to run it

Like you're fiveFirst you give the computer its crayons (install). Then you press the big button (run). Then you go look at the pictures it drew.

You need Python 3.9 or newer and SQLite 3.25 or newer, because older SQLite cannot do window functions. Both were fine on your machine.

Once only: get into the folder and install the tools

cd "C:\Users\saiak\Downloads\Food Delivery  Ride-Hailing Analytics\Food Delivery  Ride-Hailing Analytics"
pip install pandas numpy matplotlib

Run everything with one command

python run_pipeline.py

Or do just one piece

# only remake the CSV files in data/
python generate_data.py

# ask one question by hand (swap in any q01 to q14 file)
python -c "import sqlite3,pandas as pd; c=sqlite3.connect('food_ride.db'); print(pd.read_sql(open('sql/queries/q04_delivery_time_by_city.sql').read(), c))"

After it finishes, open outputs/charts/ to see the pictures and outputs/ for the answer CSVs.

The computer invents the toy city.

build_db() calls generate_data.main(). It writes seven CSV files into data/ from a fixed random seed, so you get the same 79,992 orders every run.

It throws out the old cabinet and builds a new empty one.

It deletes food_ride.db if it exists. Then it runs sql/schema.sql, which creates the 7 tables, their rules and the indexes.

It puts every notebook into the cabinet.

pandas reads each CSV and appends it into its table. This is the part that prints the row counts you saw.

cities 6 rows weather 540 rows food_orders 79,992 rows rides 48,767 rows restaurants 240 rows delivery_partners 360 rows drivers 600 rows
It asks all 14 questions, one after another.

run_queries() loops over q01…q14. Each SQL file runs against the database. The result becomes a table, is saved as a CSV in outputs/, and its first 12 rows are printed on screen. Each block starts with a line like this.

It draws seven pictures from the answers.

The questions

The 14 questions, in kid words

Like you're fiveEach file in sql/queries is one question. SQL is just a very polite way to ask the cabinet a question.
QueryQuestion for a five-year-oldSQL trick it practises
q01How many orders and rides did we get in total, and how many went wrong?UNION ALL, conditional totals
q02Which hours of the day is everybody hungry?DENSE_RANK, running share
q03Is business growing, once we smooth out the bumpy days?7-day rolling AVG, LAG
q04Which city brings dinner fastest, and which is slowest?Median and p90 by hand, SLA %
q05How much slower is dinner at rush hour than at quiet times?CASE dayparts, window total
q06Which restaurants are the top 3 in each city?DENSE_RANK … PARTITION BY
q07Who are the best delivery riders: the busiest, or the fastest?Two rankings at once
q08When do people want taxis, and when is it hard to get one?Centred 3-hour rolling average
q09Which drivers earn the most for each vehicle type?DENSE_RANK … PARTITION BY
q10Which day-and-hour combos are the hottest?Pre-aggregated CTE, window sum
q11Which days were strangely slow?Window frame on the prior 14 days, variance
q12How much did each city grow month to month?LAG, share of total
q13When taxis cost more, do fewer people get rides?Bucketing and a join to weather
q14Does rain make food late?Join, LAG over an aggregate

Demand is concentrated

Hours 12–13 and 19–21 carry 46% of all food orders.

Rush hour costs about 6 minutes

About 35 minutes at lunch and dinner against about 29 otherwise. Cancellations rise to about 3.5%.

Rain makes the bad days

40 of the 43 flagged slow city-days were rainy.

Steady growth with a weekly beat

The 7-day average goes from about 770 to about 1,025 orders a day. Weekends are about 18% higher.

Cab supply is stretched at commute time

Hours 8–9 and 17–19 average 1.39x surge and about 20% unfulfilled requests. Other hours average 1.07x and about 12%.

Surge trades completion for price

On dry days, completion falls from 88.3% to 80.8% as surge climbs to 1.5–1.99x.

Busiest is not fastest

Bengaluru's top partner by volume (894 deliveries) ranks 22nd on speed.

These are properties of the invented data, not real company results.

Dictionary

Big words made small

SQL
The language you use to ask a database questions.
SQLite
A tiny database that lives in a single file, food_ride.db.
CSV
A plain text spreadsheet. Each line is a row and commas split the columns.
Synthetic data
Pretend data that a program made up.
Pipeline
A chain of steps where each step hands its result to the next.
Window function
A way to compare each row with its neighbours, like "how does today compare with the last 7 days?"
DENSE_RANK
Gives 1st, 2nd, 3rd place. Ties share a place and nobody skips a number.
Rolling average
An average over the last few days that slides forward. It smooths bumpy lines.
p50 and p90
p50 is the middle delivery. p90 means 9 out of 10 deliveries were this fast or faster.
SLA
A promise about speed. Here it is "delivered within 30 minutes".
Surge
A price multiplier that kicks in when many people want rides at once.
GMV
Gross merchandise value: the total money that orders and fares add up to.
